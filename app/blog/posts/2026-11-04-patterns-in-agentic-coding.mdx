In October I shipped 553 pull requests on my side project, on nights and
weekends, outside my full-time job at Stripe, and over the last four months the
codebase grew by about 900,000 lines. And yes, that code is in production, not
causing massive problems, and making money, and things are only accelerating.
That would've been a completely ridiculous set of sentences a few years ago
(?!). The thing is, a lot of what we know as software engineers comes down not
to pure computer science but to impure computer engineering patterns that got
codified as best practices through _decades_ of discovery, based on the tooling
we had at the time. Coding agents have now fundamentally reopened the frontier
of the whole field, and new patterns are showing up faster than anyone is
writing them down. So I thought I'd take the time to start writing down the
ones that have worked for me in daily use and aren't gimmicks.

For two years I was merging about 14 PRs a month, and then at the end of 2025
it went kind of vertical. The codebase went the same way:

<LocChart slug="2026-11-04-patterns-in-agentic-coding" />

It took about a year and a half to get to 40,000 lines, and then it barely
moved for about six months (that's when I thought it would never make real
money and was losing interest, to be honest). Today it's about 1.4 million.

I know how that sounds, so I want to be clear: it isn't total slop. Honestly,
most of the time I think it's higher quality code than I used to write by hand,
with way more tests and way stricter rules about how things are supposed to be
built than I ever had the patience for (keep reading about my approach to
managing coding agents with lots of custom linters to understand why). Also
important to mention, it isn't useless feature slop either. It's a bunch of
features that users are actually using in prod and the company is making money
from. The amount of code I write by hand has durably gone to about zero since
last November, and a lot of my hours on the side project now are closer to being a manager of a
bunch of agents, and the patterns below are what's let me do that.

I decided I wanted to write this article when I was reading Martin Fowler's
_Refactoring_ and he referenced another book about software patterns (the Gang
of Four's _Design Patterns_, from 1994). I had never heard of it, and I was
really surprised to see that the command pattern was in there. Let's talk about
that for a sec.

The command pattern is where you make a class instead of a function, so you can
decompose the function into its constituent parts, group them under that class,
and expose an `execute` method on it. So you have a complex function that's
essentially nicely abstracted or packaged up a little cleaner than a module
file with a bunch of dumped functions in it, and you call it the same way you'd
call any function. Something like this, in Ruby:

```ruby
class ApproveLoanApplication
  def initialize(application)
    @application = application
  end

  def execute
    validate!
    offer = build_offer
    notify_merchant(offer)
    offer
  end

  private

  # def validate!, def build_offer, def notify_merchant ...
end

ApproveLoanApplication.new(application).execute
```

Whoever calls it just sees the one method, and when you open the file the
`execute` method reads kind of like a table of contents for everything below
it.

It does a lot of nice things, defining privacy clearly, letting you use object
parameters, making self-referencing methods easier, and allowing you to
leverage inherited OOP to add or expose consistent things like logging libs and
other little libraries as configurable things of all functions. Nice!

We use this all the time at Stripe. In fact, we use it so much, I'm actually a
little unsure if a bare function in a file would even compile with our internal
restrictions. I'll try that next week and let you know I guess :) Anyways, I
had no idea that this was a general software pattern that went far beyond
Stripe, let alone one with its own chapter in a book from the nineties.

It reminded me how many software patterns there are out there that I don't know
of, or that I do know of but never knew had a name, and that form the core of
my understanding of how to be a software developer anyways. It's a nice feeling
really to be like oh, yeah, you can continue learning in the space essentially
indefinitely. There are a lot of extremely interesting patterns out there at
different layers of abstraction than you might've even been thinking of.

As practitioners it's easy to at times mistakenly come to think of computer
science or computer engineering as a _somewhat_ settled science, or a somewhat
known or settled engineering field, where the "new" and trendy patterns are
just rehashing of old things (ah, a new generation of "model-view-controller").
But of course there's another side of things where you say to yourself, the
ground underneath me keeps moving so consistently and fairly fast! A new
framework! A new library! Etc. Of course, that's what many of us found so
interesting about our job, that it's not just rote memorization. I don't know,
it seems like bridge engineering might be comparably settled.

Coding agents feel different from a new framework, though. They change how the
code gets written in the first place, so a lot of the field is sort of back
open. Plenty of the old patterns are still useful for sure, and some are even
more useful now than they were (the command pattern turns out to be one of
them, more on that in #5). But there are also hundreds or thousands of new ones that nobody has
consolidated into best practices yet. Here's a first batch, from things I do at
Stripe and on my side project.

## 1. Do the giant refactor, then backfill coverage against main

You do a giant refactor and then you stop. You take the existing main branch
and your refactor branch, give them both to a coding agent, and say something
like:

> I want you to build 100% behavioral coverage of everything that I've
> refactored, but I want you to build that 100% line coverage against main.
> Think about every possible way that this refactor could fail and make sure we
> have total coverage.

Then you check that those tests pass on main, and see whether or not they fail
on your new branch. If something passes on main and fails on your branch, you
changed behavior somewhere, and now you know where.

You might think this is kind of useless, because the agent has access to
everything. It can read the refactor it's supposed to be catching, so there's
no test holdout. But in practice I've found it extremely helpful. I think it
works because the tests have to pass on main first, so they end up describing
how the old code behaved and not how the agent thinks the new code should
behave. This is one of the core things that has let me do gigantic refactors,
like 50,000-line refactors, that I don't think I'd have had the nerve to merge
otherwise.

## 2. PR walkthrough videos

This one is basically a prompt in `AGENTS.md` that tells the agent to always do
a full end-to-end manual test of whatever it built and record a video of it,
and then to add voice narration where the agent explains the walkthrough. So
now if I just go into Conductor and say "make this simple change and put up a
PR," it comes back with a guided product tour of the change.

Here's a real one, from a pretty small PR on my side project that hides the
lists a contact is already on when you go to add them to a list. Everything in
it is fake seed data. Turn your sound on:

This sounds totally crazy, but it has become one of the most turnkey things
that help me evaluate agentic PRs quickly. You can often tell that they're
wrong almost instantly without reading any code, and then you give the agent
behavioral changes based on the video. There's just something about demos, and
video and voice together, that helps you reason extremely quickly. (It's
probably the same reason a live demo in a meeting usually goes better than
everyone reading the doc.)

This has really begun redefining how I write software outside of Stripe. About
70% of my PRs on my side project have a video now. In a way it almost feels
like scrolling TikTok. You're sending ideas at a bunch of parallel coding
agents and getting back full demo videos every few minutes, over and over.

## 3. Lint for purity

One of the biggest changes I've seen is an explosion in custom linters that
enforce coding standards and the shape of whatever framework you've built. The
idea is to give agents guidance that's deterministic, so they find out they
did something wrong from a failing check, and I don't have to be the one who
tells them in review.

A good example is banning data access outside of a data access layer. In
JavaScript that can be as simple as an ESLint rule:

```js
// eslint.config.mjs
export default [
    {
        files: ["**/*.ts"],
        ignores: ["src/data/**"],
        rules: {
            "no-restricted-imports": [
                "error",
                {
                    paths: [
                        {
                            name: "src/db",
                            message: "Go through src/data instead.",
                        },
                    ],
                },
            ],
        },
    },
];
```

My side project has about 50 custom rules now, with names like
`no-supabase-or-postgres-mocking`, `no-vague-variable-names`, and
`require-useeffect-explanation`.

Before agents, we humans often skipped writing these kinds of "purity" rules
and left ourselves in a more mixed state. Somebody would query the database
from a controller once because there was a deadline, and then there were two
ways to do it, and eventually five, and everyone sort of lived with it because
writing and maintaining the rule felt like more work than it was worth. And for
people it kind of was! You could just tell the new person how things were
supposed to work.

Agents really benefit from this stuff, though, and I think they can go 10x
faster when the guardrails are strict and clear. If the questions are already settled
and there's one correct pattern, the agent doesn't have to guess which of the
five ways is the real one by reading the codebase (and it'll often pick the
wrong one, because the wrong one is usually the most common). When correctness
is automated through really expansive linting, the agent just keeps going
until everything passes, and I'm only reviewing the stuff that a check
can't catch.

The cost of writing them changed too. A custom lint rule used to be a whole
afternoon of work for something that saved you a few review comments a year.
Now an agent writes the rule in a few minutes, and then it gets enforced on
hundreds of agent PRs.

## 4. Way more integration and end-to-end tests

The same thing is happening with integration and end-to-end tests, and I think
this one goes against something a lot of us were taught.

The testing pyramid (another old pattern) says to write lots of unit tests, a
smaller number of integration tests, and only a handful of end-to-end tests at
the top, because end-to-end tests are slow, flaky, and a pain to write and keep
working. That made sense when a person had to write and maintain every one of
them. Most teams I've been on had a pretty thin layer of end-to-end tests
because of it, and we leaned on people clicking around before a release to
cover the rest.

Agents change both sides of that. Writing and fixing an end-to-end test is
cheap now, so the cost side mostly goes away. And the value side goes up,
because an agent can't look at the app the way I can unless something makes it
look. A unit test that an agent wrote for code it just wrote tends to check
that the code does what the agent thinks it does, which isn't very much
information. An end-to-end test that logs in, clicks through the flow, and
checks what's on the screen is going to fail when the feature is actually
broken, no matter who wrote it.

So I've ended up with a lot more tests at the top of the pyramid than I ever
would have written by hand, and agents run them on every change. The catch is
they're still slow, and at some point the suite gets slow enough that it's the
bottleneck. Recently I had an agent fold 1,459 end-to-end tests into 368
longer scenario tests that walk through a whole flow each, mostly to cut the
runner time in half. Having way too many end-to-end tests is a pretty funny
problem to have, given where most of us started.

## 5. Architecture for agents

I've changed my mind about a lot of architecture stuff in the last year. I used
to like frameworks that let you write less code, and I'd mostly push back on
heavy structure like domain-driven design, because all those layers and folders
are a lot of boilerplate for a person to type. But codegen is basically free
now. My running joke at this point is "zach believes DDD is important as
codegen becomes free," and I kind of mean it: the boilerplate argument mostly
goes away, and what's left is the part where everything has one obvious place
to live.

That turns out to matter way more for agents than it did for people. If there's
a model layer, a command layer (the pattern from the start of this post), a
single place for data access, and a folder structure that says which domain
owns what, then an agent can find the right spot and copy the shape of the code
already there. Even little stuff like naming matters more. I now prefer
`db.peer_fundraisers` over `db.peerFundraisers` because it matches the table
name, so an agent (or me) can grep for one string and find everything.

The other funny thing is that the migration itself got cheap. A big
restructuring like this used to be the kind of suggestion where people would
say "zach, that is an insane suggestion, please stop." Now it's more like, we
write a spec for the pattern and the folder structure, and an agent can do most
of the migration in one go, without nearly as much emotional effort as it used
to take.

## 6. Build the first one by hand

Once the PRD and the eng design are agreed on, it's really tempting to hand the
whole design doc to an agent and get the entire project back in one PR. I do
this a lot now, and it works surprisingly well. It consistently makes sense to
just go super fast and get an entire project to 80% in a single day, sometimes
10,000+ lines in one PR, merged behind a feature flag and then fixed up in
follow-ups. We have entered the yolo phase of software history, I think.

But I think it mostly works when there's already a really good example in the
codebase of the thing you're asking for. The first feature of a new kind should
get built slowly and painstakingly and reviewed line by line, because that's
the one the agent is going to copy. After that the next few similar features
can go fast, because there's actually a very good model for the AI to work off
of, in our style.

I do still draw a line on dangerous stuff. If a change touches something like
the filter compiler or anything with money, I want it in smaller pieces, even
when the agent could do it all at once.

## 7. Build the metric first

On my side project we pull in campaign contribution data from every state, and
every state does it a little differently, which makes it a great job for a big
fan-out of agents. But before I pointed agents at it I spent a while on
something pretty boring, which was an accuracy page I could actually trust. That meant fixing the
ground-truth samples (some of the hand-gathered ones were just wrong) and adding
about a thousand new samples from official disclosures.

Once that page was the north star, I opened one agent per state (something
like 44 PRs in about twenty minutes), and they all reported their results
against the same numbers. A lot of them fixed their state. Some came back with
a PR that only documented why the data couldn't be fixed yet, which honestly
was the result I wanted from those states, way more than a fake fix that looked
good.

I think the general version of this is to build the scoreboard before you fan
out. If you can't trust the metric, you just get a pile of confident PRs, and
you have no idea which of them made things worse.

## 8. Let a backtested reviewer approve PRs

At 20-ish PRs a day, I can't review everything carefully, so I built an AI
reviewer that rates every PR from 1 to 10 and is allowed to approve the safe
ones on its own. The interesting part to me is how it's kept honest. The
final rating is the lowest of a few things: the model's rubric score, a set of
hard caps from plain rules (migrations, auth, anything with money, big diffs), a
cap whenever a serious finding is still open, and a second judge that only sees
the title, description and diff. In my notes for it I wrote that "a model
rating is easy to talk up," which is the whole reason for the caps.

Then I tuned it like a model. I backtested it against the last 1,000 PRs,
including a list of known-bad PRs that had caused real problems. The setting I
landed on approves about 30% of PRs and would have let through 1 of the 7
known-bad ones. When an approved PR does cause a problem, it goes on the
known-bad list and the next backtest has to catch it.

I'm not totally sure 30% is right. I think line count is probably a bad signal
by itself, but I actually think the AI is decent at combining signals, so the
threshold is kind of a bet on that. We're not at 100%, but I think we're
probably at 30%, which is why I tuned it to 30% in the backtest.

## 9. Agents launching agents

A lot of my agent sessions aren't started by me anymore. When a ticket gets
created in Linear, it automatically turns into a Conductor workspace with an
agent working it end to end, unless the ticket says "no conductor." There's
also a Slack bot that can read the codebase and our support tickets, and if a
conversation turns into "can we fix this," it can start a coding session,
check in on it, and send it follow-ups.

The tricky part has been the handoff between them. The bot's instructions say
"Write a self-contained brief. The coding agent cannot read this thread,"
because the new agent starts with none of the conversation that led to it.
They also say "Do not infer authorization from a request to start coding,"
since an agent that's allowed to start other agents will happily read a lot of
permission into a pretty casual message.

## 10. Own the code instead of buying it

This one is less of a coding pattern and more of a business one. Because code
is so cheap now, I keep ending up replacing vendors with code we own: a CSV
import tool, a data grid library, a hosted job queue, an email editor, even our
monitoring setup. A few years ago a lot of these would've been obviously wrong
calls, the kind of thing where you'd spend a year maintaining something a
company does better.

I think the math is different now. We keep learning this, that owning the code
is good. As long as it's good code, which, you know, I'm not totally sure about
yet in every case.

## 11. Project chats

For anything bigger than one PR, I've started keeping one long-running agent
chat per project, and I kind of treat it like the eng lead on the project. It
holds the plan, splits the work into PRs, starts other agents on them, keeps
track of which ones merged and which ones are stuck, and deals with rebases and
review comments as they come in.

And then I mostly just chat with it, the same way I'd talk to a tech lead.
Where are we at? What's blocking the email stuff? Let's cut the mobile part
for now and come back to it. It goes and does that across however many PRs it
needs to, and I don't have to be the one keeping the state of 30 open PRs in my
head. That part used to be most of the work of running a project, honestly,
more than writing any of the code.

## 12. Goals that run for weeks

The next step past that is a project chat that you just let go. I give it a
constrained domain (this one part of the app, these folders, nothing else),
verification rules for what counts as done (tests pass, a walkthrough video,
the metric actually moved), and a `/goal`. Then it runs for days or weeks,
shipping PRs on its own, and when it actually needs a decision it asks me in
Slack (!).

It's a really weird feeling the first time. You get a Slack message that's
basically "hey, the old export and the new one disagree on refunded gifts, which
one is right?", you answer it from your phone, and an hour later there's a PR
for it. It feels a lot more like having a teammate than using a tool.

I think the constraints are what make it safe to leave running. The domain
keeps the blast radius small, the verification rules mean it can't just
declare victory, and the goal tells it when to stop. Without those three, a
chat that runs for a week is mostly a way to generate a lot of slop very
efficiently.

## 13. Notice when you followed Claude too far

The last one is more of a warning. Every so often I'll be a few days into an
agent-driven refactor and realize I don't really understand where it's going
anymore. Each step made sense, the agent kept proposing reasonable next steps,
and I kept saying yes, and now the code is in a state nobody actually chose.

When that happens I think the right move is to stop and just say it out loud: I
followed Claude too far. Then go back to talking it through as humans, about
where we're actually at and what end state we want, before anything else gets
written. Sometimes you just gotta go "slop!" and recalibrate.

## More to come

I'm sure there are a lot more of these, and I'm still figuring a bunch of them
out. Probably most of them will seem obvious in ten years, the way the command
pattern seems obvious to me now that I know it has a name.
