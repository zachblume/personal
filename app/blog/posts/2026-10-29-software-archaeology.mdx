---
title: "The programmer-archaeologist in the age of AI"
publishedAt: "2026-10-29"
summary: "Most of the job in a big codebase is figuring out why things are the way they are"
---

{/* chatting started me thinking */}

I was talking on a little break with a colleague that was fairly new to Stripe,
she had just joined a few months ago. We had gone away from our desks and
stepped up to the cafeteria. We often end up doing this because there's not
that many private spaces at the office other than rooms and having a chitchat
in a room is funny. The tradeoff is that you're in a weirdly empty giant lunch
room. It's still nice though. Some of my most memorable conversations happened
this way -- with people passing through town from another office, or at some
kind of lifecycle stage of work life so to speak (just joined, about to leave,
right after something stressful, etc.)

We were talking about how work changes as you go from well scoped tasks to more
ambiguous things. At Stripe we have team and organization-level weekly meetings
carved out for engineering design reviews, which are like an academic peer
review space for documents and plans. In fact, this academic culture -- the
practice of peer review -- is my favorite thing about Stripe. I think a lot of
people will say, "the people" are their favorite thing about a company, and I
know what they mean -- to me, this practice is probably the most important way
that technical relationships between people are formed, or at least its one of
the most positive and interesting ways that happens. People get a lot smarter
through this process -- or at least I know that I did.

She was saying how she found the design discussions really interesting, that a
room of people who each know some corner of the system really deeply, each with
only a partial picture, and be sitting there disagreeing and talking it through
live.

In one recent review, many of us were surprised to here that a core type of our
system was nullable (or "nilable" in Stripe's Ruby parlance). Bizarre! This is
a thing we interact with everyday and is fundamentally un-nullable on the
object it sits on, but we accidentally (?) have been marking it so to users.
Actually, most of the traffic for our subproduct (loans) relies on embedded
react components rather than APIs, so our teams APIs as of yet are under a bit
less scrutiny than the billions-of-dollars-per-day core payments APIs of
Stripe. Because of that it wasn't like, my-world-turned-upside-down surprising,
but it _was_ surprising -- that we didn't know this detail of our own system!

{/* book */}

It reminded me of Vernor Vinge's _A Fire Upon the Deep_. I read this book
because of how often it comes up on hackernews -- like many probably have. The
book opens with a team of human researchers at a lab out on the edge of the
galaxy, digging through an archive that's been sitting dormant for something
billions of years. They don't really understand what's in it. What they've
actually dug up is the Blight, an incredibly hostile superintelligence. (That's
the first few pages. It gets _much_ weirder.) Vinge mentions the name of the
job in that book but gives a fuller lift to the job in the prequel, _A Deepness
in the Sky_: "a programmer-archaeologist."

Thousands of years in the future, ships run on software stacked on software
stacked on software, nobody writes anything from scratch anymore, and the most
valuable people on board are the ones who can dig down through the layers and
figure out what something actually does. There's this great detail where the
ship's clock counts seconds from what everyone assumes is the first moon
landing, but if you dig all the way down it's actually the Unix epoch, and they
didn't know what that was.

Nobody is going to eat the galaxy over a nilable field, but: somebody made a
decision a long time ago, it made sense to them, and the code survived but the
reason didn't.

{/* side project vs. stripe */}

My side project is over a million lines at this point and it still mostly fits
in my head. When something looks weird I usually remember why I did it, or at
least the rough shape of why, and that's enough to get going. Stripe is
something like 50 million lines of Ruby. Nobody has that in their head -- nobody
is even close, not even the people who have been here for a decade. And I don't
think that's just the same job but bigger, I think it's sort of a different job.
At a million lines you're mostly an author. At fifty million you're mostly a
reader, and you're reading stuff written by people you'll probably never meet,
for reasons that were obvious to them at the time and are not at all obvious to
you.

{/* meaning drift */}

The weird part (to me at least) is that the code doesn't really change that
much, but what it _means_ does. It goes something like: a field gets added with
a name that perfectly describes what it holds. Then a second product starts
writing to it for a slightly different reason. Then there's a migration and
half the old rows mean one thing and half mean another. Then someone adds a
flag that changes how it gets read. The name never changes, so the code still
reads totally clearly -- it's just clearly saying something that stopped being
true a couple years ago. I don't actually know the full history of our nilable
field yet, but I'd bet money it's something like that.

It's kind of like reading an old book where the words have drifted. "Nice" used
to mean foolish, for instance. You can read every word on the page and still
misunderstand the sentence, because you're reading it in today's language and
it was written in an older one.

{/* digging */}

So the actual work ends up looking a lot like digging. You `git blame` a line,
and the first commit you hit is almost always useless -- a formatter, a rename,
someone moving a file from one folder to another. So you keep going. Maybe
`git log -S` to find when a string first showed up. You find the PR, the PR
links a ticket, the ticket links a design doc, the design doc mentions an
incident, and the incident writeup finally says the one thing you needed to
know. Each layer is someone's decision that made sense at the time, sitting on
top of someone else's decision that made sense at the time. Sometimes the
answer isn't written down at all and you end up DMing someone who worked on it
four years ago and hoping they remember (they usually sort of do).

{/* mental model as cache */}

Once you've done the dig, you sort of cache it. I think that's what people
actually mean when they talk about having a "mental model" of a big system:
it's a pile of cached excavations. You don't redo the dig every time you touch
that code, you just remember what you found last time. A lot of what makes a
senior engineer valuable on a big codebase isn't that they're smarter, it's
that their cache is bigger -- or at least that's a big part of it. Which also
means it goes stale, because other people are adding layers on top of the
layers you dug through, and it turns out cache invalidation is just as hard
inside your head as it is everywhere else.

{/* underrated skill */}

I think this is a pretty underrated skill and it's mostly not taught. School
and bootcamps and side projects teach you to write code, almost always in
codebases small enough that you're the author of most of it. Then you show up
somewhere big and most of the job is reading, and nobody really told you that
reading is a craft with its own techniques. The newer engineers I've seen get
good fast are usually the ones who get curious about the history instead of
annoyed by it -- which is part of why I liked hearing that my colleague found
the design reviews interesting rather than frustrating. They tend to assume the
previous person was smart and had constraints they can't see yet. (They're
usually right. And sometimes the previous person was them.)

{/* ai tools */}

So where does AI fit into this? It helps a lot, honestly. An agent can grep
fifty million lines way faster than I can, and it's really good at summarizing
a messy git log or chasing a PR to a ticket to a doc. A lot of the shovel work
of the dig is just getting cheaper, and I think that's great.

But it's sort of the situation the researchers in _A Fire Upon the Deep_ are
in. They have amazing automation that can decode the archive and run it, layer
by layer, and it all seems to work -- it just can't tell them what any of it
_means_. The meaning-drift problem is exactly the thing the tools are worst at,
because they read the code in today's language, same as a new engineer does.
The field is called what it's called, so that's what it must hold. And a lot of
the real context was never written down anywhere in the first place. It lived
in a meeting, or a Slack thread, or someone's head, and that person left two
reorgs ago.

If anything I think AI makes the archaeology part of the job _more_ important,
not less. We're about to have way more code, written way faster, by people (and
agents) who spent way less time thinking about each line. That's a lot of new
strata. As long as software outlives the people who wrote it, which is
basically always, someone is going to have to be the programmer-archaeologist.

{/* ending */}

Anyways -- someday someone is going to `git blame` your code, squint at it, and
wonder what on earth you meant. Leave them a good commit message. It might be
you.
