{/* chatting started me thinking */}

I was talking on a little break with a colleague that was fairly new to Stripe,
she had just joined a few months ago. We had gone away from our desks and
stepped up to the cafeteria. We often end up doing this because there's not
that many private spaces at the office other than rooms and having a chitchat
in a room is funny. The tradeoff is that you're in a weirdly empty giant lunch
room. It's still nice though. Some of my most memorable conversations happened
this way -- with people passing through town from another office, or at some
kind of lifecycle stage of work life so to speak (just joined, about to leave,
right after something stressful, etc.)

We were talking about how work changes as you go from well scoped tasks to more
ambiguous things. At Stripe we have team and organization-level weekly meetings
carved out for engineering design reviews, which are like an academic peer
review space for documents and plans. In fact, this academic culture -- the
practice of peer review -- is my favorite thing about Stripe. I think a lot of
people will say, "the people" are their favorite thing about a company, and I
know what they mean -- to me, this practice is probably the most important way
that technical relationships between people are formed, or at least its one of
the most positive and interesting ways that happens. People get a lot smarter
through this process -- or at least I know that I did.

She was saying how she found the design discussions really interesting, that a
room of people who each know some corner of the system really deeply, each with
only a partial picture, and be sitting there disagreeing and talking it through
live.

In one recent review, many of us were surprised to here that a core type of our
system was nullable (or "nilable" in Stripe's Ruby parlance). Bizarre! This is
a thing we interact with everyday and is fundamentally un-nullable on the
object it sits on, but we accidentally (?) have been marking it so to users.
Actually, most of the traffic for our subproduct (loans) relies on embedded
react components rather than APIs, so our teams APIs as of yet are under a bit
less scrutiny than the billions-of-dollars-per-day core payments APIs of
Stripe. Because of that it wasn't like, my-world-turned-upside-down surprising,
but it _was_ surprising -- that we didn't know this detail of our own system!

{/* book */}

It reminded me of Vernor Vinge's _A Fire Upon the Deep_. I read this book
because of how often it comes up on hackernews -- like many others, probably. The
book opens with a team of human researchers at a lab out on the edge of the
galaxy, digging through an archive that's been sitting dormant for something
billions of years. They don't really understand what's in it. What they've
actually dug up is the Blight, an incredibly hostile superintelligence. (That's
the first few pages. It gets _much_ weirder.) Vinge mentions the name of the
job in that book but gives a fuller lift to the job in the prequel, _A Deepness
in the Sky_: "a programmer-archaeologist."

Thousands of years in the future, ships run on software stacked on software
stacked on software, nobody writes anything from scratch anymore, and the most
valuable people on board are the ones who can dig down through the layers and
figure out what something actually does. There's this great detail where the
ship's clock counts seconds from what everyone assumes is the first moon
landing, but if you dig all the way down it's actually the Unix epoch, and they
didn't know what that was.

Nobody is going to eat the galaxy over a nilable field, but: somebody made a
decision a long time ago, it made sense to them, and the code survived but the
reason didn't.

{/* side project vs. stripe */}

My side project has gotten pretty big, but I wrote all of it, so when
something looks weird it's usually because I did it and I can sort of remember
why. Stripe is something like 50 million lines of Ruby, and nobody has that in
their head, including the people who have been here for a decade. So a lot of
the job is figuring out why something is the way it is, and then explaining it
to other people.

{/* archaeologist: the dig */}

Figuring it out usually starts with `git blame`. The first commit you hit is
almost always a formatter run or someone moving the file, so you keep going.
`git log -S` will find when the thing first showed up, and from there you can
usually follow the PR to a ticket and the ticket to a design doc. If you're
lucky there's an incident writeup or a Slack thread at the end where someone
says the thing you needed to know. If you're not, it was never written down,
and you DM someone who worked on it four years ago and hope they remember. They
usually sort of do.

{/* you: the real nilable-field dig (or another) -- what you found, dead ends */}

{/* historian: explaining it */}

The explaining part takes up more of my time than the digging. People ask me
why something works the way it does, or whether it's safe to change, and a lot
of the time I know because I've been around that part of the system long
enough to have already done the dig.

{/* you: the kinds of questions people bring you */}

The design review my colleague liked is this happening live. Each
person in the room knows some part of the history, and the discussion is them
putting it together. What gets passed around is latent knowledge, meaning
things that are true about the system but aren't written in the code. Some of
it is in old PR comments or Slack, but most of it is in people's heads.

{/* cognitive debt */}

I don't think this is good. I think code should be self-documenting, and this
part of my job is a big reason I believe that. The nilable field is a good
example. The code was documenting itself, since the type said nilable, but what
it said was wrong. The people who knew better learned it somewhere other than
the code.

This has always been best practice. If you leave latent knowledge in a
codebase, everyone after you has to become a programmer-archaeologist to work
on it. We might call this cognitive debt, which is everything you need
to know to safely change the code that the code itself doesn't tell you. You
pay for it when people leave and take what they knew with them, and when new
people take months to get up to speed.

With people the cost is at least bounded. Someone does the dig once, remembers
what they found, and becomes the person you ask about that part of the system.
As long as they stick around, it stays paid.

{/* ai */}

I use agents for a lot of this digging now. They're good at grepping across 50
million lines and summarizing a long git log, which saves me hours.

{/* you: what you actually use them for at work */}

Anyone who's set up an agent with RAG has seen that it does better the more it
can read. If you give it your Slack and Google Docs it does really well, and
that's most of why every company is pushing enterprise search right now. It's a
way of making latent knowledge explicit enough for an agent to find.

{/* you: a time an agent nailed it because it found the thread or doc */}

Indexing Slack only gets you partway, though. Slack threads go stale the
same way code does, and an agent will read the type and believe it. It would
have told me the field was nilable. Agents also don't remember anything between
sessions, so each one does the whole dig again, and none of them can DM the
person who left. We've never had collaborators we could run this many copies
of, so we've never paid cognitive debt this often. It used to cost us something
each time a new person joined, and now it costs us on every task an agent picks
up.

{/* the end of the programmer-archaeologist */}

There's something kind of ironic here with Vinge. In _Deepness_ the
programmer-archaeologist is a job of the far future, thousands of years out,
and the archaeologists are some of the most valuable people on the ship. The
book is set in a part of the galaxy where real AI doesn't work, so people are
still the ones doing the digging, with machines helping. In real life it's a
job we've had for a while now, and I think it's the thing that's starting to
fade.

To be fair, he did kind of see the slop coming. The whole reason his ships need
archaeologists is that the software has piled up into something nobody can
understand anymore. But I think a couple things are playing out differently in
real life.

One is speed. His ships pile up software over thousands of years, slowly enough
that a few skilled people can keep up with it. If you allow
programmer-archaeologist behavior in your codebase and then 100x your velocity
with agents, you can pile up thousands of years of layers in a year or two.
Every agent that misreads the nilable field writes new code on top of the
misreading, and that new code has its own latent knowledge that nobody holds.
No human can reconstitute it as fast as it's being made.

The other is that the layers aren't human anymore. When I dig through old
Stripe code, I'm reconstructing decisions people made. Somebody had a reason,
it was written down somewhere or it's in someone's head, and if I'm lucky I can
DM them. AI slop doesn't have that. There's no decision to recover and nobody
to ask. Trying to understand it feels less like being a programmer-archaeologist and more
like being a mathematician working through machine code.

So the speedup ends up forcing the issue. If you want to keep moving that fast,
you have to make the code self-documenting much sooner than you otherwise
would. That means code that says what it means (starting with types that are
right) and boundaries that let you or an agent change one part without knowing
the history of everything around it. If you don't, the codebase turns into slop
and your velocity goes with it, and I think that's close to an existential risk
compared to your peers who did the work. Someone will still have to dig
sometimes, but I don't think every senior engineer will need to be an
archaeologist to get work done.

This is a little funny for me, because being one of those programmer-archaeologists is a lot
of my job right now. The next part of the job is making that less necessary.

{/* ending */}

Anyways, someday someone is going to `git blame` your code and wonder what you
meant, and it might be an agent doing it a thousand times a day. People used to
say to leave a good commit message. I don't think that's enough anymore. The
code should explain itself, so nobody has to go digging.
