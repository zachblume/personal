title: "The programmer-archaeologist in the age of AI"
publishedAt: "2026-10-29"
summary: "Most of the job in a big codebase has been figuring out why things are the way they are. AI changes that."

A lot of working in a big codebase is figuring out why things are the way they
are. You dig through old PRs and design docs and Slack threads, and then you
explain what you found to whoever's asking. Over time you piece together how
the code got that way. Vernor Vinge has a name for this, the
programmer-archaeologist, and in his books it's a job
of the far future. I've been thinking about it a lot lately, because I think AI
is changing this job pretty fast, and it's going a lot differently than he
imagined. Being an archaeologist has been part of being an engineer, but I
don't think we can keep working this way with agents writing so much code.

## Wait, that's nilable?

I was talking on a little break with a colleague who was fairly new to Stripe;
she had just joined a few months ago. We had gone away from our desks and
stepped up to the cafeteria. We often end up doing this because there aren't
that many private spaces at the office other than meeting rooms, and having a
chitchat in a meeting room is funny. The tradeoff is that you're in a weirdly
empty giant lunch room. It's still nice though. Some of my most memorable
conversations happened this way -- with people passing through town from
another office, or people who'd just joined, were about to leave, or had just
gotten through something stressful.

We were talking about how work changes as you go from well scoped tasks to more
ambiguous things. At Stripe we have team and organization-level weekly meetings
where we review engineering designs and plans, a bit like academic peer
review. In fact, this academic culture -- the practice of peer review -- is my
favorite thing about Stripe. I think it's one
of the most important ways people learn how their colleagues think about
engineering, and people get a lot smarter through it -- or at least I know
that I did.

She was saying how she found the design discussions really interesting: a room
of people who each know some corner of the system really deeply, each with only
a partial picture, sitting there disagreeing and talking it through live.

In one recent review, many of us were surprised to hear that a core type of our
system was nullable (or "nilable" as we call it in Stripe's Ruby). Bizarre!
This is a thing we interact with every day, and it can never actually be nil
on the object it sits on, but we've accidentally (?) been telling users it could be
nil. Actually, most of the traffic for our product (loans) goes through
embedded React components rather than APIs, so our team's APIs are under a bit
less scrutiny so far than the billions-of-dollars-per-day core payments APIs of
Stripe. Because of that it wasn't like, my-world-turned-upside-down surprising,
but it _was_ surprising -- we didn't know this detail of our own system!

## A Fire Upon the Deep

It reminded me of Vernor Vinge's _A Fire Upon the Deep_. I read it because of
how often it comes up on Hacker News, like a lot of people probably did. The
book opens with a team of human researchers at a lab out on the edge of the
galaxy, digging through an archive that's been sitting dormant for billions of
years. They don't really understand what's in it, but they have really good
automation that can decode it and run it, so they do. What they've actually dug
up is the Blight, an incredibly hostile superintelligence. (That's the first
few pages. It gets _much_ weirder.)

The prequel, _A Deepness in the Sky_, is where the job I'm talking about gets
its name: "programmer-archaeologist." Thousands of years in the future, ships
run on software stacked on software stacked on software, nobody writes anything
from scratch anymore, and the most valuable people on board are the ones who
can dig down through the layers and figure out what something actually does.
There's this great detail where the ship's clock counts seconds from what
everyone assumes is the first moon landing, but if you dig all the way down
it's actually the Unix epoch, and nobody remembers what that was.

Nobody is going to eat the galaxy over a nilable field, but somebody made a
decision a long time ago, it made sense to them, and the code survived but the
reason didn't.

## Little and big codebases

My side project has gotten pretty big (1m lines), but I wrote most of it and
all of it for the first 30% of the project, so when something looks weird it's
usually because I did it, and I can sort of remember why. Stripe is something
like 50 million lines of Ruby, and nobody has that in their head, not even the
people who've been here for a decade. So a lot of the job ends up being
figuring out why something is the way it is, and then explaining that to other
people.

Figuring it out usually starts with git blame, and the first commit you hit
is almost always a formatter run or someone moving the file to a new folder, so
you keep going. git log will find when the thing first showed up, and from
there you can usually follow the PR to a ticket and the ticket to a design doc.
If you're lucky there's an incident writeup or a Slack thread at the end where
someone finally says the thing you needed to know. If you're not lucky, it was
never written down anywhere, and you DM someone who worked on it four years ago
and hope they remember (they usually sort of do).

## What people know

In my experience the explaining part takes up more time than the digging.
People ask why something works the way it does, or whether it's safe to change,
and a lot of the time whoever's been around that part of the system the longest
already knows, because they figured it out years ago.

The design review my colleague liked is pretty much this happening live.
Everyone in the room knows some part of the history, and the discussion is them
putting it together out loud. They know things about the system that you can't
learn from reading the code. Some of it's in old PR comments or Slack, but most
of it is just in people's heads.

## Cognitive debt

I don't think this is good! I think code should be self-documenting, and this
part of the job is a big reason I believe that. The nilable field is a good
example, because the code _was_ documenting itself (the type said nilable), it
was just saying the wrong thing. Everyone who knew better learned it somewhere
other than the code.

This is why we're always told to write self-documenting code. If understanding
it requires knowing things that only exist in someone's head or an old Slack
thread, everyone after you has to become a programmer-archaeologist just to
work on it. We might call this cognitive debt (not quite in the sense people
have been using it lately, for offloading your thinking to ChatGPT): everything
you need to know to safely change the code that the code itself doesn't tell
you. You pay for it when people leave and take what they knew with them, and
when new people take months to get up to speed.

People at least remember what they figure out. The next time it comes up, you
can ask them instead of going through the same old PRs again.

## Agents doing the digging

Agents are pretty good at a lot of this digging already. They can grep across
50 million lines and summarize a long git log way faster than I can.

Anyone who's given an agent access to their team's docs has seen how much that
helps. If you give it your Slack and your Google Docs it does really well,
and that's a big part of why so many companies are pushing enterprise search
right now. An agent can look up the explanation in an old thread instead of
having to guess from the code.

Indexing Slack only gets you partway, though. Slack threads go stale the same
way code does, and an agent will read the type and believe it. It would have
told me the field was nilable, no questions asked. Agents also don't remember
anything between sessions, so each one does the whole dig over again, and none
of them can DM the person who left. A new engineer has to learn this history
when they join. With agents, we're repeating that work on every task, sometimes
with dozens of agents doing it at once.

## The past and future of slop

There's something ironic here with Vinge. In _Deepness_ the
programmer-archaeologist is a job of the far future, thousands of years out,
and they're some of the most valuable people on the ship. (The book is set in a
part of the galaxy where real AI doesn't work, so people are still the ones
doing the digging, with machines helping.) In real life it's a job we've had
for a while now, and I think it's starting to fade.

To be fair, he did kind of see the slop coming. The whole reason his ships need
archaeologists is that the software has piled up into something nobody
understands anymore. I just think a couple things are playing out differently
in real life.

One is speed. His ships pile up software over thousands of years, slowly enough
that a few skilled people can keep up. If your code already takes this much
digging to understand, and then you write it 100 times faster with agents, you
can pile up thousands of years of layers in a year or two. Every agent that misreads the
nilable field writes new code based on that mistake. Now you have to understand
the mistake to make sense of the new code, and nobody can figure all of this
out as fast as the agents are writing it. Which is pretty much the opening of
_A Fire Upon the Deep_ again: really good automation running something nobody
understands, one layer at a time.

The other is that people didn't write this code. When I dig through old
Stripe code I'm reconstructing decisions people made. Somebody had a reason, it
got written down somewhere or it's in someone's head, and if I'm lucky I can DM
them. With AI slop, there's nobody to ask why it was written that way. You end
up tracing what it does line by line and trying to decide whether any of it
makes sense.

If you want to keep writing code that fast, you have to make the code
self-documenting a lot sooner than you otherwise would: code that says what
it means (starting with types that are right), and
boundaries that let you or an agent change one part without knowing the history
of everything around it. Someone will still have to dig sometimes, but I don't
think every senior engineer will need to be an archaeologist just to get work
done. And if you don't do it, the codebase turns into slop and you slow down
again. I think a company could fall so far behind competitors who do this work
that it can't catch up.

This is a little funny for me, because being one of those
programmer-archaeologists is a lot of my job right now, and I think the next
part of the job is making that less necessary.

Anyways, someday someone is going to git blame your code and wonder what you
meant, and it might be an agent doing it a thousand times a day. People used to
say to leave a good commit message. I don't think that's enough anymore. The
code should really just explain itself.
