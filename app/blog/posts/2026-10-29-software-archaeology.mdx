{/* chatting started me thinking */}

I was talking on a little break with a colleague that was fairly new to Stripe,
she had just joined a few months ago. We had gone away from our desks and
stepped up to the cafeteria. We often end up doing this because there's not
that many private spaces at the office other than rooms and having a chitchat
in a room is funny. The tradeoff is that you're in a weirdly empty giant lunch
room. It's still nice though. Some of my most memorable conversations happened
this way -- with people passing through town from another office, or at some
kind of lifecycle stage of work life so to speak (just joined, about to leave,
right after something stressful, etc.)

We were talking about how work changes as you go from well scoped tasks to more
ambiguous things. At Stripe we have team and organization-level weekly meetings
carved out for engineering design reviews, which are like an academic peer
review space for documents and plans. In fact, this academic culture -- the
practice of peer review -- is my favorite thing about Stripe. I think a lot of
people will say, "the people" are their favorite thing about a company, and I
know what they mean -- to me, this practice is probably the most important way
that technical relationships between people are formed, or at least its one of
the most positive and interesting ways that happens. People get a lot smarter
through this process -- or at least I know that I did.

She was saying how she found the design discussions really interesting, that a
room of people who each know some corner of the system really deeply, each with
only a partial picture, and be sitting there disagreeing and talking it through
live.

In one recent review, many of us were surprised to here that a core type of our
system was nullable (or "nilable" in Stripe's Ruby parlance). Bizarre! This is
a thing we interact with everyday and is fundamentally un-nullable on the
object it sits on, but we accidentally (?) have been marking it so to users.
Actually, most of the traffic for our subproduct (loans) relies on embedded
react components rather than APIs, so our teams APIs as of yet are under a bit
less scrutiny than the billions-of-dollars-per-day core payments APIs of
Stripe. Because of that it wasn't like, my-world-turned-upside-down surprising,
but it _was_ surprising -- that we didn't know this detail of our own system!

{/* book */}

It reminded me of Vernor Vinge's _A Fire Upon the Deep_. I read this book
because of how often it comes up on hackernews -- like many others, probably. The
book opens with a team of human researchers at a lab out on the edge of the
galaxy, digging through an archive that's been sitting dormant for something
billions of years. They don't really understand what's in it. What they've
actually dug up is the Blight, an incredibly hostile superintelligence. (That's
the first few pages. It gets _much_ weirder.) Vinge mentions the name of the
job in that book but gives a fuller lift to the job in the prequel, _A Deepness
in the Sky_: "a programmer-archaeologist."

Thousands of years in the future, ships run on software stacked on software
stacked on software, nobody writes anything from scratch anymore, and the most
valuable people on board are the ones who can dig down through the layers and
figure out what something actually does. There's this great detail where the
ship's clock counts seconds from what everyone assumes is the first moon
landing, but if you dig all the way down it's actually the Unix epoch, and they
didn't know what that was.

Nobody is going to eat the galaxy over a nilable field, but: somebody made a
decision a long time ago, it made sense to them, and the code survived but the
reason didn't.

{/* side project vs. stripe */}

My side project is over a million lines at this point and it still mostly fits
in my head. When something looks weird I usually remember why I did it, or at
least the rough shape of why. Stripe is something like 50 million lines of
Ruby. Nobody has that in their head -- nobody is even close, not even the
people who have been here for a decade. So the job ends up splitting into two
things: digging up why something is the way it is, and then explaining it to
everyone else. The archaeologist and the historian, so to speak.

{/* archaeologist: the dig */}

The digging part goes something like this. You `git blame` the line, and the
first commit you hit is almost always useless -- a formatter, a rename, someone
moving the file from one folder to another. So you keep going. `git log -S` to
find when the thing first showed up, then the PR, then the ticket the PR links
to, then the design doc the ticket links to, and if you're lucky an incident
writeup or a Slack thread where someone finally says the one thing you actually
needed to know. Sometimes you're not lucky and it was never written down at
all, and you end up DMing someone who worked on it four years ago and hoping
they remember (they usually sort of do).

{/* you: the real nilable-field dig (or another) -- what you found, dead ends */}

{/* historian: explaining it */}

The second part, being the historian, is honestly the bigger one. Good senior
engineers at big companies spend a surprising amount of their time just
_explaining the history_ -- why this is like this, what it used to mean, who
decided, whether it's safe to change. I'm like that. If I'm being honest it's a
big part of what I'm valued for.

{/* you: the kinds of questions people bring you */}

And that design review my colleague liked is basically this happening live. A
room of historians, each holding a partial picture of the system, putting it
together out loud. What's getting passed around in that room is latent
knowledge: stuff that's true about the system but isn't anywhere in the code.
It lives in old PR comments and incident docs and Slack threads, and mostly in
people's heads.

{/* cognitive debt */}

I don't actually think this is good! I think code _should_ be
self-documenting, and this part of my job is a big reason why I believe that.
The nilable field is a pretty sharp example of the problem, because the code
_was_ documenting itself -- the type said nilable -- it was just documenting
the wrong thing. Everyone who knew better knew it from somewhere other than the
code.

This was always best practice, too. If you leave latent knowledge sitting in
the codebase, then everyone who comes after you has to become a
programmer-archaeologist just to work on it. I've started thinking of it as
cognitive debt -- like tech debt, but instead of being the stuff you'd have to
_change_ to work on the code, it's the stuff you'd have to _know_. And it has
real costs: knowledge leaves when people leave, new people are slow, and the
same decisions get re-litigated in design review because nobody can find where
they were made the first time.

But with humans the cost was at least sort of bounded. Somebody does the dig
once, remembers what they found, and then they become the historian for that
corner of the system. The debt gets paid down a little every time someone
learns it, and it mostly stays paid as long as they stick around.

{/* ai */}

Which brings me to AI. I use agents for a lot of this digging now and they're
genuinely great at the shovel work -- grepping across 50 million lines,
summarizing a messy git log, chasing a PR to a ticket to a doc.

{/* you: what you actually use them for at work */}

And I think we all kind of know from our little agents doing RAG that the more
is written down, and the more of it they can reach, the better they do. If you
give it your Slack and your Google Docs it does super well. That's basically
the whole enterprise search push happening in the industry right now: take the
latent stuff and make it explicit enough that an agent can find it.

{/* you: a time an agent nailed it because it found the thread or doc */}

But indexing Slack is sort of a patch. Slack threads go stale and drift just
like code does, and an agent will read the type and believe it -- it would've
told you with total confidence that the field is nilable. And more importantly,
agents don't remember. Every session starts from zero. Each one pays for the
whole dig again, and none of them can DM the person who left. We never had
infinitely scalable collaborators before, so we never really paid cognitive
debt at this rate. It used to be something like an onboarding tax, and now
it's a tax on every single task. I think it was always one of the main limiting
factors on how fast you could move in a big codebase -- we just felt it slowly,
one new hire at a time.

{/* the end of the programmer-archaeologist */}

So I think the era of the programmer-archaeologist is sort of coming to a
close. Someone will still have to dig sometimes. But I don't think it's going
to be something every senior engineer has to be just to get stuff done,
because the AI speedup works like a ratchet: once agents are doing a lot of the
work, you're kind of forced to take that latent knowledge and materialize it
into the code itself. Types that are actually true. Tests that enforce the
thing everyone "just knows." And good boundaries, so that you (or an agent) can
work on one piece without needing to know the history of the whole system
around it. That's what the agents can actually use. And if you don't do it, I
think that's close to an existential risk for your codebase's velocity versus
your peers. The teams that do this are just going to move faster, and that
compounds.

It's a funny inversion of Vinge, actually. In _Deepness_ the
programmer-archaeologists are the most valuable people on the ship, basically
forever. Maybe we're the ones who get to not need them.

The uncomfortable part, for me at least, is that being the historian is a big
part of what I'm valued for, and I think the job now is to write myself out of
it.

{/* ending */}

Anyways -- someday someone is going to `git blame` your code and wonder what on
earth you meant. It might be you, or it might be an agent doing it a thousand
times a day. The old advice was to leave them a good commit message, and I
don't really think that cuts it anymore. Nobody should have to go digging
through the history to find out what your code means. It should just say it.
