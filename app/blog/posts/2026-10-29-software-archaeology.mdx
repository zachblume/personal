{/* chatting started me thinking */}

I was talking on a little break with a colleague that was fairly new to Stripe,
she had just joined a few months ago. We had gone away from our desks and
stepped up to the cafeteria. We often end up doing this because there's not
that many private spaces at the office other than rooms and having a chitchat
in a room is funny. The tradeoff is that you're in a weirdly empty giant lunch
room. It's still nice though. Some of my most memorable conversations happened
this way -- with people passing through town from another office, or at some
kind of lifecycle stage of work life so to speak (just joined, about to leave,
right after something stressful, etc.)

We were talking about how work changes as you go from well scoped tasks to more
ambiguous things. At Stripe we have team and organization-level weekly meetings
carved out for engineering design reviews, which are like an academic peer
review space for documents and plans. In fact, this academic culture -- the
practice of peer review -- is my favorite thing about Stripe. I think a lot of
people will say, "the people" are their favorite thing about a company, and I
know what they mean -- to me, this practice is probably the most important way
that technical relationships between people are formed, or at least its one of
the most positive and interesting ways that happens. People get a lot smarter
through this process -- or at least I know that I did.

She was saying how she found the design discussions really interesting, that a
room of people who each know some corner of the system really deeply, each with
only a partial picture, and be sitting there disagreeing and talking it through
live.

In one recent review, many of us were surprised to here that a core type of our
system was nullable (or "nilable" in Stripe's Ruby parlance). Bizarre! This is
a thing we interact with everyday and is fundamentally un-nullable on the
object it sits on, but we accidentally (?) have been marking it so to users.
Actually, most of the traffic for our subproduct (loans) relies on embedded
react components rather than APIs, so our teams APIs as of yet are under a bit
less scrutiny than the billions-of-dollars-per-day core payments APIs of
Stripe. Because of that it wasn't like, my-world-turned-upside-down surprising,
but it _was_ surprising -- that we didn't know this detail of our own system!

{/* book */}

It reminded me of Vernor Vinge's _A Fire Upon the Deep_. I read this book
because of how often it comes up on hackernews -- like many probably have. The
book opens with a team of human researchers at a lab out on the edge of the
galaxy, digging through an archive that's been sitting dormant for something
billions of years. They don't really understand what's in it. What they've
actually dug up is the Blight, an incredibly hostile superintelligence. (That's
the first few pages. It gets _much_ weirder.) Vinge mentions the name of the
job in that book but gives a fuller lift to the job in the prequel, _A Deepness
in the Sky_: "a programmer-archaeologist."

Thousands of years in the future, ships run on software stacked on software
stacked on software, nobody writes anything from scratch anymore, and the most
valuable people on board are the ones who can dig down through the layers and
figure out what something actually does. There's this great detail where the
ship's clock counts seconds from what everyone assumes is the first moon
landing, but if you dig all the way down it's actually the Unix epoch, and they
didn't know what that was.

Nobody is going to eat the galaxy over a nilable field, but: somebody made a
decision a long time ago, it made sense to them, and the code survived but the
reason didn't.

{/* side project vs. stripe */}

- side project is 1M+ lines, still mostly fits in my head
- stripe is ~50M lines of ruby, nobody has it in their head, not even people
  who've been here 10 years
- so the job splits in two: digging up why something is the way it is
  (archaeologist), and explaining it to everyone else (historian)

{/* archaeologist: the dig */}

- walk through one real dig, ideally the nilable field
- `git blame` -> first hit is a formatter / rename / file move
- `git log -S` -> PR -> ticket -> design doc -> incident writeup / slack thread
- [you: what you actually found, and the dead ends -- author left, doc 404s,
  ticket system migrated]
- sometimes it's not written down anywhere and you DM someone who worked on it
  4 years ago

{/* historian: explaining it */}

- the other half, and honestly the bigger one: being the person who can
  _explain_ the history
- good senior engineers at big companies do this constantly. I'm like that,
  it's a big part of what I'm valued for
- [you: the questions people bring you -- "why does X...", "is it safe to
  change Y"]
- that design review my colleague liked is exactly this: a room of historians,
  each holding a partial picture, putting it together live
- what's getting passed around is latent knowledge: stuff that's true about the
  system but isn't in the code
- [you: where it actually lives -- old PR comments, incident docs, slack,
  specific people's heads]

{/* cognitive debt */}

- I don't think this is good! code _should_ be self-documenting, and this job
  is a big part of why I believe that
- nilable field is the sharp example: the code _was_ documenting itself -- the
  type said nilable -- it just documented the wrong thing
- this was always best practice: if you leave latent knowledge in the codebase,
  other people have to become programmer-archaeologists to work on it
- call it cognitive debt: like tech debt, but it's what you have to _know_ to
  work on the code, not what you have to change
- costs: knowledge leaves when people leave, new people are slow, decisions get
  re-litigated in design review
- but with humans it was bounded: someone digs once, remembers, becomes the
  historian

{/* ai */}

- [you: what you actually use AI for when digging at work]
- great at the shovel work: grep across 50M lines, summarize a git log, chase
  PR -> ticket -> doc
- and we all know from our little agents doing RAG: the more is written down
  and reachable, the better they do. give it slack + google docs and it does
  super well -> that's the whole enterprise search push
- [you: a time an agent nailed it because it found the thread / doc]
- but indexing slack is a patch. threads go stale and drift just like code. an
  agent reads the type and believes it -- it would've told you the field is
  nilable
- and agents don't remember. every session starts from zero, pays for the dig
  again, can't DM the person who left
- we never had infinitely scalable collaborators before, so we never paid this
  debt at this rate. it goes from an onboarding tax to a per-task tax
- it was always the limiting factor, we just felt it slowly

{/* the end of the programmer-archaeologist */}

- so I think the era of the programmer-archaeologist is sort of coming to a
  close
- someone will still have to dig sometimes, but it won't be something every
  senior eng has to be just to get stuff done
- the AI speedup is a ratchet: it forces us to materialize latent knowledge
  into better structures (types, tests, docs that live next to the code)
  because that's what agents can actually use
- if you don't, it's like an existential risk for codebase velocity vs. your
  peers -- the ones who do it move faster, and it compounds
- inversion w/ Vinge: in _Deepness_ the archaeologists are the most valuable
  people on the ship, forever. maybe we get to not need them
- uncomfortable bit for me: being the historian is what I'm valued for, and the
  job now is to write myself out of it

{/* ending */}

- someone will `git blame` your code someday -- might be you, might be an agent
  doing it a thousand times a day
- leave a good commit message. better yet, make the code say it
