{/* chatting started me thinking */}

I was talking on a break with a colleague that was fairly new to Stripe, a few
months. We had gone away from our desks and stepped up to the cafeteria. I
often end up doing this with folks because there's not that many private spaces
at the office. The tradeoff is that you're in a weirdly empty giant lunch room.
It's still nice though.

We were talking about how the work changes as you go from well scoped tasks to
more ambiguous things. At Stripe we have team and organization-level spaces for
engineering design reviews, which are like an academic peer review space for
documents and plans. In fact, this academic culture and practice of peer review
is my favorite thing about Stripe.

She found the design discussions really intersting, that a room of people who
each know some corner of the system really deeply, each with only a partial
picture, are sitting there disagreeing and talking it through.

In one recent one, many of us were surprised to here that a core type of our
system was nullable (or "nilable" in Stripe's Ruby parlance). Bizarre! This is
a thing we interact with everyday and is fundamentally un-nullable on the
object it sits on, but we accidentally (?) have been marking it so to users.
Actually, most of the traffic for our subproduct (loans) relies on embedded
react components rather than APIs, so we are under a bit less scrutiny than the
billions-of-dollars-per-day core payments APIs of Stripe, so it wasn't like,
end-of-the-world surprising, but it was surprising -- we didn't know this
detail of our own system!

{/* side project */}

My side project is over a million lines at this point and it still mostly fits
in my head. When something looks weird, I usually remember why I did it. Even
when I don't remember exactly, I remember the shape of the decision, and that's
usually enough.

Stripe is something like 50 million lines of Ruby. Nobody has that in their
head. Nobody is even close. And I don't think that's the same job at a bigger
scale -- it's a different job. At a million lines you're mostly an author. At
fifty million you're mostly a reader, and you're reading things written by
people you'll never meet, for reasons that were obvious to them and are not
obvious to you.

Here's the part that took me a while to notice: the code doesn't change, but
what it means does.

A field gets added with a name that perfectly describes what it holds. Then a
second product starts writing to it, for a slightly different reason. Then a
migration happens and half the old rows mean one thing and half mean another.
Then someone adds a flag that changes how it's read. The name never changes,
so the code still reads perfectly clearly. It's just clearly saying something
that stopped being true a couple years ago.

It's a bit like reading an old book where the words have drifted. "Nice" used
to mean foolish. You can read every word on the page and still completely
misunderstand the sentence, because you're reading it in today's language and
it was written in an older one. Old code is the same. You have to figure out
what the words meant when they were written, and then trace how they got to
what they mean now.

So the actual work looks like digging. You `git blame` a line, and the first
commit you hit is almost always useless -- a formatter, a rename, someone
moving a file. You keep going. `git log -S` to find when a string first showed
up. You find the PR, and the PR links a ticket, and the ticket links a design
doc, and the design doc mentions an incident, and the incident writeup finally
says the thing you needed to know. It's strata. Each layer is someone's
decision that made sense at the time, sitting on top of someone else's.

And then once you've done the dig, you cache it. That's what having a "mental
model" of a big system actually is, I think: a pile of cached excavations. You
don't redo the dig every time you touch that code, you just remember what you
found. A lot of what makes a senior engineer valuable on a big codebase isn't
that they're smarter, it's that their cache is bigger.

The problem is that it's a cache, so it goes stale. The system keeps changing
underneath you. Other people are adding layers on top of the layers you
excavated. Cache invalidation being one of the two hard problems in computer
science applies to the inside of your head too.

I think this is a pretty underrated skill, and it's mostly not taught. School
and bootcamps and side projects teach you to write code. Nearly all of them
put you in codebases small enough that you're the author. Then you show up
somewhere big and most of the job is reading, and nobody told you that reading
is a craft with its own techniques. The newer engineers I've seen get good fast
are usually the ones who get curious about the history instead of annoyed by
it. They assume the previous person was smart and had constraints they can't
see yet, rather than assuming the previous person was dumb. (They're usually
right. And sometimes the previous person was them.)

AI tools help with this, to be clear. An agent can grep fifty million lines
faster than I can, and it's great at summarizing a messy git log. But the
meaning-drift problem is pretty much exactly the thing they're bad at. They
read the code in today's language, same as a new engineer does. And a lot of
the real context was never written down anywhere -- it lived in a meeting, or
a Slack thread, or someone's head, and that person left. A better shovel
doesn't change the fact that you're digging. As long as software outlives the
people who wrote it, which is basically always, I think this stays a permanent
part of the job.

Someday someone is going to `git blame` your code, squint at it, and wonder
what on earth you meant. Leave them a good commit message.
