---
title: "The programmer-archaeologist in the age of AI"
publishedAt: "2026-10-29"
summary: "Most of the job in a big codebase is figuring out why things are the way they are"
---

{/* chatting started me thinking */}

I was talking on a little break with a colleague that was fairly new to Stripe,
she had just joined a few months ago. We had gone away from our desks and
stepped up to the cafeteria. We often end up doing this because there's not
that many private spaces at the office other than rooms and having a chitchat
in a room is funny. The tradeoff is that you're in a weirdly empty giant lunch
room. It's still nice though. Some of my most memorable conversations happened
this way -- with people passing through town from another office, or at some
kind of lifecycle stage of work life so to speak (just joined, about to leave,
right after something stressful, etc.)

We were talking about how work changes as you go from well scoped tasks to more
ambiguous things. At Stripe we have team and organization-level weekly meetings
carved out for engineering design reviews, which are like an academic peer
review space for documents and plans. In fact, this academic culture -- the
practice of peer review -- is my favorite thing about Stripe. I think a lot of
people will say, "the people" are their favorite thing about a company, and I
know what they mean -- to me, this practice is probably the most important way
that technical relationships between people are formed, or at least its one of
the most positive and interesting ways that happens. People get a lot smarter
through this process -- or at least I know that I did.

She was saying how she found the design discussions really interesting, that a
room of people who each know some corner of the system really deeply, each with
only a partial picture, and be sitting there disagreeing and talking it through
live.

In one recent review, many of us were surprised to here that a core type of our
system was nullable (or "nilable" in Stripe's Ruby parlance). Bizarre! This is
a thing we interact with everyday and is fundamentally un-nullable on the
object it sits on, but we accidentally (?) have been marking it so to users.
Actually, most of the traffic for our subproduct (loans) relies on embedded
react components rather than APIs, so our teams APIs as of yet are under a bit
less scrutiny than the billions-of-dollars-per-day core payments APIs of
Stripe. Because of that it wasn't like, my-world-turned-upside-down surprising,
but it _was_ surprising -- that we didn't know this detail of our own system!

{/* book */}

It reminded me of Vernor Vinge's _A Fire Upon the Deep_. I read this book
because of how often it comes up on hackernews -- like many probably have. The
book opens with a team of human researchers at a lab out on the edge of the
galaxy, digging through an archive that's been sitting dormant for something
billions of years. They don't really understand what's in it. What they've
actually dug up is the Blight, an incredibly hostile superintelligence. (That's
the first few pages. It gets _much_ weirder.) Vinge mentions the name of the
job in that book but gives a fuller lift to the job in the prequel, _A Deepness
in the Sky_: "a programmer-archaeologist."

Thousands of years in the future, ships run on software stacked on software
stacked on software, nobody writes anything from scratch anymore, and the most
valuable people on board are the ones who can dig down through the layers and
figure out what something actually does. There's this great detail where the
ship's clock counts seconds from what everyone assumes is the first moon
landing, but if you dig all the way down it's actually the Unix epoch, and they
didn't know what that was.

Nobody is going to eat the galaxy over a nilable field, but: somebody made a
decision a long time ago, it made sense to them, and the code survived but the
reason didn't.

{/* side project vs. stripe */}

My side project is over a million lines at this point and it still mostly fits
in my head. When something looks weird I usually remember why I did it, or at
least the rough shape of why, and that's enough. Stripe is something like 50
million lines of Ruby. Nobody has that in their head, nobody is even close. And
I don't think that's the same job just bigger -- I think it's sort of a
different job. At a million lines you're mostly an author. At fifty million
you're mostly a reader, and you're reading stuff written by people you'll never
meet, for reasons that were obvious to them and aren't obvious to you.

{/* meaning drift */}

The weird part is that the code doesn't really change, but what it means does.
A field gets added with a name that perfectly describes what it holds. Then a
second product starts writing to it for a slightly different reason. Then a
migration happens and half the old rows mean one thing and half mean another.
Then someone adds a flag that changes how it's read. The name never changes, so
the code still reads totally clearly -- it's just clearly saying something that
stopped being true a couple years ago. I don't actually know the full history
of our nilable field yet, but I'd bet money it's something like that.

It's kind of like reading an old book where the words have drifted. "Nice" used
to mean foolish. You can read every word on the page and still misunderstand
the sentence, because you're reading it in today's language and it was written
in an older one.

{/* digging */}

So the actual work looks a lot like digging. You `git blame` a line, and the
first commit you hit is almost always useless -- a formatter, a rename, someone
moving a file. So you keep going. `git log -S` to find when a string first
showed up. You find the PR, the PR links a ticket, the ticket links a design
doc, the design doc mentions an incident, and the incident writeup finally says
the thing you needed to know. Each layer is someone's decision that made sense
at the time, sitting on top of someone else's.

{/* mental model as cache */}

Once you've done the dig, you sort of cache it. I think that's what a "mental
model" of a big system actually is: a pile of cached excavations. You don't redo
the dig every time you touch that code, you just remember what you found. A lot
of what makes a senior engineer valuable on a big codebase isn't that they're
smarter, it's that their cache is bigger. Which also means it goes stale --
other people are adding layers on top of the layers you dug through -- and
cache invalidation turns out to be just as hard inside your head.

{/* underrated skill */}

I think this is a pretty underrated skill and it's mostly not taught. School
and bootcamps and side projects teach you to write code, almost always in
codebases small enough that you're the author. Then you show up somewhere big
and most of the job is reading, and nobody told you that reading is a craft
with its own techniques. The newer engineers I've seen get good fast are
usually the ones who get curious about the history instead of annoyed by it,
which is part of why I liked that my colleague found the design reviews
interesting rather than frustrating. They assume the previous person was smart
and had constraints they can't see yet. (They're usually right. Sometimes the
previous person was them.)

{/* ai tools */}

AI tools help with this a lot. An agent can grep fifty million lines faster
than I can, and it's great at summarizing a messy git log. But honestly that's
sort of the Straumli researchers' situation -- their automation was amazing at
decoding and running the archive, it just couldn't tell them what it meant. The
meaning-drift problem is exactly the thing the tools are worst at, because they
read the code in today's language, same as a new engineer does. And a lot of
the real context was never written down anywhere. It lived in a meeting, or a
Slack thread, or someone's head, and that person left. As long as software
outlives the people who wrote it, which is basically always, I think this is
just part of the job.

{/* ending */}

Anyways -- someday someone is going to `git blame` your code, squint at it, and
wonder what on earth you meant. Leave them a good commit message. It might be
you.
